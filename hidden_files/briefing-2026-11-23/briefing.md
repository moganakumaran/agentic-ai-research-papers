## 2. Trend signal
Three threads are converging. (a) **Memory is now evaluated as a liability surface, not just an asset**: StaleBench, Experience Rot, WikiTrace and HEAMS all show memory hurting — via stale retrieval, silent change, wrong lessons, or governance failures — and the fixes are gating mechanisms (scope, evidence links, provenance), not bigger stores. (b) **Benchmark validity is being quantified, not just asserted**: Benchmarks' Last Exam (29.3% validity for multi-step suites), SplitBench (graders gameable), and the LLM-judge label-bias paper (arXiv:2611.26881) give Paper #1's corrected-label argument independent company. (c) **Failure diagnosis is becoming executable**: ProbeAgent turns failure modes into tests that can confirm recurrence — the method Paper #1's taxonomy work lacks. The open contest is now: RANGE-style claims that scoped failure lessons help (general agentic tasks, under review) vs the growing "memory hurts unless gated" evidence — and nobody has settled it for data agents, where the failure modes are execution-verifiable.

## 3. Gap tags (what each strong paper does NOT solve)
- **ProbeAgent**: diagnoses failures in verifiable environments only; no data/SQL domain, no memory intervention tested — it finds modes, it does not test whether remembering them helps.
- **StaleBench**: factoid currency (Wikidata/TempLAMA) only; no procedural/schema drift, no Text-to-SQL, no cost accounting for the fix.
- **RAG in a Trenchcoat**: memory benchmarks are QA-style, not agentic data tasks; probes are synthetic (5×20 scenarios); no repeated-run reliability (pass^k) reported.
- **How Much Should an Agent Remember?**: AppWorld only; guidelines are LLM-consolidated (provenance silently dropped — their own reported failure); no compute-matched comparison across memory sizes.
- **Experience Rot**: silent *tool-interface* change, not schema/data drift; no remediation that works (announcement and doc-pointing both fail); no memory-invalidation design proposed.
- **Benchmarks' Last Exam**: a synthesis/audit, not a new benchmark; no data-agent benchmark in the eight reviewed; no fix for the multi-step validity gap beyond process recommendations.
- **AskWise**: clarification, not memory; 120 sales tasks + AmbiQT slice only; no repeated-task / drift setting.
- **Do Senior Agents…**: role integrity in institutional hierarchies, not data-agent pipelines; LLM-judge rubric calibrated to humans, but judge bias (see 2611.26881) is a confound.
- **Who Grades the Agent?**: coding-agent benchmarks only; SplitBench blocks 75% of adversarial attempts — 25% still get through.

## 4. Paper #1 angle
**Implication**: StaleBench's headline — "retrieval metrics can rank memory policies backwards" — is the independent precedent for Paper #1's central methodological claim. Our A3 original-label win (+9.8 pts) that vanished on corrected labels is the same phenomenon in data agents. Draft v2 should cite StaleBench (and its Contamination@5) as the general-domain analogue, and adopt its framing: report the corrected-label metric as primary and the original-label metric as the artefact demonstration, not the reverse.
**Experiment it motivates**: the model-dependence finding ("How Much Should…") plus Experience Rot jointly predict a *capability × staleness* interaction — memory helps strong models on stable tasks, hurts under silent change. Our null is on gpt-5-mini + Qwen2.5-Coder-32B; the discriminating test is the frontier A1 run (critique item M4, ~$12 for 1 run): if GPT-5 A1−A0 > 0 while mini/Qwen stay null, the paper's claim becomes "memory benefit is capability-gated", a stronger and more publishable result than a flat null. If GPT-5 also nulls, the null generalises across capability — also worth reporting.
